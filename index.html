How this is worked out — and what it can't know

Worth reading once, so you know how much to trust the colours.
  • Every exercise is mapped to the muscles it works, weighted — 1.0 where it's the primary target, less where it's assisting. Your logged sets become effective sets per muscle.
  • Fatigue then decays over a recovery window that differs by muscle: 72 hours for large, high-damage muscles like quads, hamstrings, glutes, lats and chest; 60 hours for mid-sized ones; 48 hours for small, high-tolerance ones like the rotator cuff and tibialis. Muscle protein synthesis runs elevated for roughly 36–48 hours after training, and 48–72 hours between sessions on the same muscle is the standard recommendation.
  • Returning-to-training adjustment. For the first four weeks the windows are stretched by 25%, because a novel or resumed stimulus causes more disruption and needs longer. It switches itself off from week 5.
  • Sessions are assumed to happen at 19:15, since that's your slot. Log on the day you train and the timing will be right.
  • What it cannot know: your sleep, your stress, how close to failure you actually went, or whether something hurts. It is a bookkeeping model, not a physiological reading. A joint that hurts overrides a green muscle, every time — especially your shoulders.
Recovery windows informed by: muscle protein synthesis elevated ~36–48 h post-session, shorter in trained lifters and longer in untrained; 48–72 h between sessions targeting the same muscle group is the common recommendation; weekly set targets from the resistance-training volume literature, where the dose-response runs to roughly 20 sets per muscle per week. Longer recovery for beginners reflects that a novel stimulus causes greater homeostatic disruption.
