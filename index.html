How this is worked out — and what it can't know

Worth reading once, so you know how much to trust the colours.
  • Every exercise is mapped to the muscles it works, weighted — 1.0 where it is the primary target, less where it assists. Logged sets become effective sets per muscle, scaled by how hard you rated the session and by how costly that kind of movement is to recover from.
  • Fatigue then fades over a window that differs by muscle: 72 hours for large muscles like quads, hamstrings, glutes, lats and chest; 60 hours for mid-sized ones; 48 hours for small, fast-recovering ones like the cuff, soleus and tibialis. Recovery is slow for the first day and faster after — the way soreness actually behaves.
  • Three things stretch the window: the first four weeks back (+25%), short sleep on the nights since (+10% under 7.5 h, +20% under 6.5 h), and a session that pushed a muscle past what it can usefully recover from.
  • Attribution. For each muscle the map keeps track of which exercise, on which day, the remaining fatigue came from — so it can tell you what to trim next time rather than only that something is tired.
  • What it cannot know: stress, illness, or whether something hurts — unless you tell it. That is what the soreness buttons and the joint check are for. It is a bookkeeping model, not a physiological reading. A joint that hurts overrides a green muscle, every time — especially the shoulders.
Recovery windows informed by: muscle protein synthesis elevated ~36–48 h post-session, shorter in trained lifters and longer in untrained; 48–72 h between sessions on the same muscle is the common recommendation; weekly set targets from the resistance-training volume literature, where the dose-response runs to roughly 20 sets per muscle per week.
